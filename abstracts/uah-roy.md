Complex systems often achieve robustness, efficiency, and adaptability without centralized control. System-level capabilities emerge from the organization of local interactions, feedback, and adaptation across multiple scales. This talk explores how these principles can inform the engineering of adaptive networked systems, drawing on examples from biological, technological, and healthcare systems. We begin with transcriptional regulatory networks, where recurring network motifs and their higher-order organization reveal structural principles associated with efficiency, robustness, and response to perturbations, motivating bio-inspired approaches to distributed sensing, in which local feedback and self-regulation enable sensor networks to balance energy consumption, information throughput, and quality of service. We extend these ideas to healthcare, where decentralized and federated learning provides a framework for collaborative intelligence under constraints of privacy, data ownership, heterogeneity, and trust. Finally, we consider systems in which adaptation occurs across coupled network layers, focusing on the interplay between information diffusion and infectious disease transmission. We develop a multiplex network framework in which social influence propagates through an inferred social network while disease dynamics evolve through a physical-contact network governed by epidemic dynamics. This coupling provides a computational framework for studying behavioral response, quarantine, and epidemic control when the underlying social and physical networks are only partially observable. Across these domains, we conclude that a common principle emerges: effective adaptation depends not only on individual components, but on how they are organized and how they interact with one another and their environment.
